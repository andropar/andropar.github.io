About

I am currently pursuing a PhD in computational cognitive neuroscience at the Max Planck Institute for Human Cognition and Brain Science in Leipzig, as well as at the University of Gießen, under the supervision of Martin Hebart. My research focuses on advancing the methodological foundations of vision science through computational and statistical approaches.

The central aim of my doctoral work is to enhance the efficiency of data acquisition in large-scale deep phenotyping studies. This pursuit encompasses two fundamental questions: first, whether it is possible to record neural responses in the human visual system at temporal frequencies substantially higher than those currently considered feasible; and second, whether computational methods can be employed to strategically pre-select visual stimuli that maximize the information gained from each experimental session.

Throughout my research, I employ functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) as the primary measurement modality, integrating techniques from computational modeling and machine learning to address these methodological challenges.

Academic Background

I obtained a Master of Science degree in Computer Science from Leipzig University, where my coursework emphasized data science, machine learning, medical image processing, and neuroscience. My master's thesis, conducted at the Max Planck Institute for Cognitive and Brain Sciences under the guidance of Martin Hebart and Katja Seeliger, investigated methods for generating visual stimuli designed to elicit maximal activation in specified cortical regions, utilizing deep neural network models of the human visual system.

Prior to my master's studies, I completed a Bachelor's degree in Business Information Systems, also at Leipzig University, which provided a foundation in both technical and organizational aspects of information technology.

Research Interests

Current
High-temporal-resolution fMRI — Investigating the feasibility of dramatically increasing the sampling rate of functional neuroimaging to capture fine-grained temporal dynamics of visual processing.
Current
Optimal stimulus selection — Developing computational frameworks for the intelligent selection of experimental stimuli to maximize information gain in large-scale neuroimaging studies.
Ongoing
Deep phenotyping initiatives — Contributing to comprehensive mapping efforts such as the THINGS initiative, which aims to characterize human visual representations across thousands of object categories.
